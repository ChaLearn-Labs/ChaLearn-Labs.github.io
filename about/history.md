ChaLearn's roots go back to a **feature selection challenge at NIPS 2003**, organized by Isabelle Guyon and collaborators, which brought together 75 participants to tackle problems from cancer diagnosis to handwritten digit recognition. The success of that event led to a series of follow-up challenges on performance prediction and model selection (2006), and on learning causal relationships (2008), each pairing a competition with a workshop and, eventually, a book.

**ChaLearn was formally incorporated in 2011** as a California tax-exempt 501(c)(3) nonprofit, to give this growing activity a permanent home. That same period saw the KDD Cup 2009 challenge on customer relationship management with Orange, the Active Learning Challenge, and the Yahoo! Learning to Rank Challenge — all drawing thousands of participants worldwide. To document the lessons learned from these competitions, ChaLearn launched the **Challenges in Machine Learning (CiML)** book series, starting with the Microtome series (2011–2014) and continuing with a Springer series (2015–2020).

Through the 2010s, ChaLearn's challenge portfolio diversified significantly:

- **Gesture recognition and Looking at People (LAP)**, starting in 2011, grew into an ongoing series of challenges on computer vision and human behavior analysis, still active today.
- **The Higgs Boson Challenge (2014)**, run with data released from CERN, launched a long-running series of challenges in high-energy physics.
- **The AutoML challenge series**, started in 2015, has driven research on machine learning systems that require no human intervention, and continues today with events like the Kaggle AutoML Grand Prix.
- **Causality and neural connectomics challenges** extended ChaLearn's early causality work into neuroscience and network reconstruction.

More recently, ChaLearn has continued to grow into new domains: **L2RPN**, run with the French grid operator RTE, challenges participants to control real-world power networks; **Fair Universe**, co-organized with Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory since 2023, studies bias in physics simulations; and **MediChal** brings the same challenge methodology to medical applications. Throughout, ChaLearn has supported the open-source **Codabench** platform — the successor to Codalab — so that organizers everywhere can run code-submission competitions without building their own infrastructure.

Today, ChaLearn's challenges and workshops remain a regular presence at venues like NeurIPS, CVPR, and ICCV, continuing a tradition that started with a single feature selection challenge more than twenty years ago.
